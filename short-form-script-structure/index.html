The parts of a short-form video script

Every short-form video that works runs on the same skeleton: a hook, a setup, a set of beats, a turn where it becomes something else, and a close. Naming each part on its own makes it possible to tell which one a script is missing when it is not landing.

What is a hook?

The hook is the opening of a short-form video — the first thing it says or shows. Its job is to stop someone from scrolling past, before they know anything else about the video. The most common way a hook fails is opening on context instead of a claim or a question: introducing yourself, thanking the platform, or explaining what the video is about before saying the thing that would make someone stay.

What is the setup?

The setup is what comes right after the hook, before the video gets into its main beats. Its job is to give the hook enough context to make sense — who is talking, why, or what is at stake — without spending so long on it that the hook's promise goes cold. The most common way a setup fails is running too long, so it becomes a second hook the video never needed.

What are the beats?

The beats are the individual steps the video moves through to deliver on what the hook promised, in the order they come in. Each beat's job is to move the video forward — a new step, a new reveal, a new piece of the demonstration — rather than repeat the one before it. The most common way beats fail is running out of order relative to the hook's promise, so a beat that should resolve tension shows up before the tension is built.

What is the turn?

The turn is the point where a video becomes something other than what it looked like it was going to be — a reveal, a reversal, a shift from problem to solution. Its job is to re-earn attention partway through, when a viewer's interest is most likely to drop. The most common way a turn fails is arriving too late, after a viewer has already decided nothing is going to change and scrolled on.

What is the close?

The close is the last beat of a video — how it wraps up once the turn has landed. Its job is to leave the viewer with the takeaway the whole video was building toward, cleanly, rather than trailing off. The most common way a close fails is ending on the same energy as the middle of the video, so nothing marks the moment as the ending.

Where does the call to action go?

A call to action is the specific thing the video asks the viewer to do — comment, follow, click, buy — and it sits inside or immediately after the close, not bolted on as an afterthought. Its job is to convert the attention the rest of the video earned into one concrete action. The most common way it fails is showing up disconnected from what the video was actually about, so it reads as a generic ask rather than the natural next step of the video.

How do you tell whether a script has all of them?

Read the script back and label each line with the part it belongs to: hook, setup, beat, turn, close, call to action. A script missing a part usually shows up as a specific symptom — no turn reads as flat, no setup reads as confusing, no close reads as unfinished. If a part is genuinely missing rather than just short, that is usually the part worth rewriting first.
