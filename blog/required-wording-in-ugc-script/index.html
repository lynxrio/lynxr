How do you add the brand's required wording to a script naturally?

Keep the brand's required wording exactly as written and change everything around it instead. Give each fixed line a moment in the script where it answers something the viewer has just seen, lead into it with a short line in your own voice, and deliver it at your normal pace rather than reading it out.

Published 18 November 2026

Can you change the brand's required wording to make it sound better?

Do not change a required line, even slightly, unless the brand agrees to a new version in writing first.

A line the brief marks as fixed is there for a reason you may not see: approval from another team, a promise the brand has made elsewhere, or a phrase it uses everywhere. Swapping one word can send the video back for a reshoot. So treat the wording as locked and treat its surroundings as yours. If a line truly will not sit in your mouth, ask the brand before you film whether a softer version is allowed, and keep the reply with your notes.

This is a different job from squeezing several selling points into a short video. Fitting the brand's key points into a short UGC script covers ideas you may phrase your own way. Required wording is the narrower case: the words themselves are fixed.

Where in the script should a required line go?

Put each required line straight after the moment that makes it relevant, so it lands as an answer rather than an announcement.

Read each fixed line and ask what the viewer would need to have just seen for it to make sense. A line about how a product feels belongs after the close-up. A line about what is in the box belongs after you open it. Then place it in that beat, not in the opening line, unless the brief says otherwise, because the opening has to stay about the viewer. How do you lead into a fixed line so it sounds like you?

Write a short bridge in your own words just before the fixed line, so your voice carries the viewer into it.

A required line sounds scripted when it arrives cold, straight after a different thought. A bridge gives it a reason to be said. Keep bridges short and plain, and make them about what you noticed, not about the product's claims.

  • Point at the screen: here is the bit I actually checked, then the fixed line.
  • Admit you are quoting: the box puts it like this, then the fixed line. This works when the wording is formal, because you are not pretending it is how you talk.
  • Ask the viewer's question for them: so what is it actually made of, then the fixed line.
  • Follow it with a short line of your own, such as that was the part I cared about, so you end the beat in your own voice.
  • Cut any bridge that repeats the fixed line in other words, since saying it twice makes both versions sound rehearsed.

How do you deliver a fixed line on camera without reading it?

Learn the fixed line until you can say it while doing the action, and film that beat as its own short take.

  • Say the line out loud while doing the action several times before you record, because your hands give your voice a rhythm to follow.
  • Film the beat with the fixed line separately, so a stumble costs you one short take, not the whole video.
  • Keep your pace the same as the lines around it. Slowing down for the fixed line is what makes it sound like an advert.
  • Check the take against the brief word by word before you move on, since a small slip is easy to miss on playback.

What should you send the brand with the script?

Send the script with every required line highlighted, so the brand can confirm the wording before you film.

A highlighted line lets the person approving the script check it in seconds and see that the rest is yours. Add a short note such as: Required lines are highlighted and copied exactly from the brief dated [DATE]. Let me know if any wording has changed. That catches an updated brief before it turns into a reshoot.
