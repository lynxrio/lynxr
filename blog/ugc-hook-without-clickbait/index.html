How do you write a UGC hook that isn't clickbait?

A UGC hook stops being clickbait when it makes one specific promise that the rest of the video visibly keeps. Write the payoff first, then write a hook that points at it, and check that a viewer who watches to the end gets exactly what the opening line suggested, no bigger and no vaguer.

Published 12 November 2026

What makes a hook feel like clickbait?

A hook feels like clickbait when the opening promises more than the video delivers, so the viewer feels tricked by the end instead of rewarded.

The problem is the gap, not the energy. A bold hook is fine if the video backs it up, and a quiet one can still mislead. Watch for three warning signs in your own drafts: a claim about everyone, such as a line saying every viewer needs this; a secret or shock that the video never reveals; and a result the product cannot honestly show on camera. The post on hook and payoff explains why a mismatch loses the viewer. This answer is about writing the line so the mismatch never happens.

Why should you write the payoff before the hook?

Write the payoff first because the hook is a promise, and you can only make an honest promise once you know what you are able to show.

  • Look at the brief and your footage plan, and find the single moment a viewer would screenshot. That moment is your payoff.
  • Describe that moment in one plain sentence, such as the lid clicking shut and the bottle staying dry upside down in a bag.
  • Now write a hook that asks the question the payoff answers. If the payoff is a dry bag, the hook can be about a leaky bag.
  • Read the hook and the payoff out loud one after the other. If they sound like the same story, you are close.

Working this way keeps you inside what the product can show, which matters for brand work because the brief limits what you can claim. The guide on writing a hook covers the openers themselves; use it once you know the payoff.

How do you test that the video keeps the hook's promise?

Test the promise by writing the hook as a question, then finding the exact line or shot in your script that answers it. If you cannot point to one, the hook overreaches.

Run the check on the finished edit too. Watch only the first line and the last shot, back to back. If a stranger would feel the second answers the first, the hook is honest.

What does an honest hook look like in a full script?

An honest hook names a real, small problem the creator has, and the video ends on a shot that shows how that problem went for them.

The hook promises one thing, a notebook that stays dry, and the close shows exactly that. It says nothing about every bag or every commute, only what this creator saw.

How do you fix a hook that promises too much?

Fix an overreaching hook by making it smaller and more personal, not by padding the video. Swap claims about everyone for one thing that happened to you.

  • Swap a universal claim for your own experience, so a line about what everyone gets wrong becomes what you kept getting wrong.
  • Replace a vague secret with the actual detail, so the viewer knows what they are waiting for.
  • Cut words like best, only and never unless the video proves them, because each one raises the bar for the payoff.
  • If the brand asks for a stronger line, offer two versions and let the brand choose, the way the answer on hooks for ads suggests testing options.
