How do you reply when a brand asks for your rates first?

Reply to a brand that asks for your rates first by thanking them, asking a few short questions about the job, and promising a quote once you have the answers. Scope decides the work, so a quote built on it is one you can stand behind, and the questions show the brand you take the job seriously.

Published 24 November 2026

Why ask about the job before you give a rate?

Asking first lets you quote for the actual job, instead of guessing and then having to walk the number back.

Two requests that both say "one video" can be very different pieces of work. One may need a single talking clip filmed at home, another several hooks, raw footage and the right to run it as an ad. A rate given before you know which can leave you either underquoted or looking expensive for a simple job. The brief, or the questions that stand in for one, is what turns a vague request into something you can price. Once you have the details, turning a brand brief into a script shows how to read them for the work involved, and the answer on working out what to charge covers building the number itself.

What should you ask the brand before you quote?

Ask only what changes the price: how many videos, what goes in them, how the brand will use them and by when.

Question to askWhy it changes the quote
How many videos, and how many versions of the hook?Each extra hook is more filming and editing
Is there a script, or do you want me to write one?Writing the script is a separate piece of work
Do you need raw footage as well as the edit?Raw files take time to sort and hand over
Where will the videos run, and for how long?Paid use is worth agreeing separately from an organic post
When do you need the finished files?A short deadline can mean moving other work

Keep the list short. Four or five questions show you know the work, while a long questionnaire can make a small job feel like a lot of effort for the brand.

What does a good reply look like?

A good reply is warm, short and specific: it thanks the brand, asks your questions in a list and says when the quote will follow.

  • Hi [NAME], thanks for reaching out about [PRODUCT], I would love to make this with you.
  • So I can send you an accurate quote, could you tell me how many videos you need, whether you have a script, whether you want raw footage, and where the videos will run?
  • If you have a brief or a reference video, send that over and I can work from it.
  • Once I have those details I will send a quote with what is included the same day.

Only promise a turnaround you can keep. If you can't reply the same day, name the [DATE] you will. Ending on a clear next step keeps the thread open, which is the point of the reply.

What if the brand still wants a number first?

Give a starting point for a clearly defined basic package, so the brand has a figure to work with and you have not committed to the full job.

Spell out what the starting rate covers, such as one edited video, one hook and organic use only, and say that extras are quoted once you know the scope. A rate card makes this easy, because you can send the basic package and keep the add-ons separate. You could write: my starting rate for one edited video with one hook for organic use is [AMOUNT]. Extra hooks, raw footage and paid use are priced on top, and I can confirm the total once I know what you need.

When should you walk away instead of quoting?

Walk away when the brand will not share any detail about the job, because you cannot price or plan work that has no shape.

Refusing to say how many videos or how they will be used makes the job hard to quote fairly. Before you decide, check the request against the signs of a deal that isn't worth taking. If you pass, do it politely and leave the door open, so the brand can come back with a clearer request later. A single line works: thanks for thinking of me, I will need a little more detail to quote, so feel free to get back in touch when the brief is ready.
