How do you write a UGC script with no talking?

Write a UGC script with no talking as a list of actions, each paired with one short line of on-screen text. The hands, the product and the text carry the message together, so every beat must show something new and every caption must be readable at a glance, with the sound off.

Published 17 November 2026

What changes when a UGC script has no talking?

The words move from your mouth to the screen, so each line has to be short enough to read while something else is happening.

In a spoken script, you can explain while you show. Without a voice, the viewer reads and watches at the same time, and long text loses that race. That means fewer words overall, more of the story told by your hands, and a clear order the eye can follow. Write your draft in the layout from the two-column script, with the action on the left and the on-screen text on the right, because a silent video is really two tracks that have to line up.

Before you start, check the brief for anything the brand wants said. A required line still has to appear, so you will place it as text instead of speech.

How do you lay out the beats of a silent script?

Give each beat one action, one short caption and one clear change on screen, and write them in the order the viewer will see them.

A beat in a silent video is the moment something visibly happens: a lid comes off, a hand swaps one item for another, a before shot cuts to an after. If two beats look the same, the viewer has no reason to keep watching, so make each one a different shot or a different movement.

How short should the on-screen text be?

Short enough to read twice before the shot changes, which means a handful of words per caption and one idea at a time.

Read each caption aloud at a normal pace while you watch the shot it sits on. If you can't finish it before the cut, trim the words or hold the shot longer. A few rules keep the text readable:

  • Put one idea in each caption, because two ideas make the viewer pick one and miss the other.
  • Leave out anything the picture already shows, so the text adds information instead of repeating it.
  • Keep captions in the same part of the frame, so the viewer's eye doesn't have to hunt for them.
  • Keep text clear of the product and your hands, because those are the parts of the shot that matter.
  • Write in your own words, as you'd text a friend, so it still sounds like a person and not a label.

The first caption does the most work, so try a few of the hook shapes from how to write a UGC hook that stops the scroll as text before you pick one.

What does a silent UGC script look like written out?

How do you keep the message clear without a voice?

Film the steps in order, show the product's name or label clearly once, and let the result sit on screen long enough to register.

A spoken video can recover from a confusing shot with a line of explanation. A silent one can't, so plan the shots before the words. Use the script as a shot list and film each beat as its own clip, which also makes it easier to swap a caption later without refilming.

  • Show the problem in a way that needs no explaining, such as a mess, a struggle or a missing piece.
  • Hold the turn shot for a beat longer than feels natural, because it's the moment the viewer needs to understand.
  • Let the product appear clearly and in good light at least once, so the viewer knows what to look for.
  • Put the call to action in text over the last shot, in the brand's own wording if the brief gives one.

If the brief lists several points, choose the one or two the picture can show best and put the others in the caption. The answer on fitting the brand's key points into a short script helps you decide which ones earn a beat.

Should you still check the script with the brand before filming?

Send the brand a two-column table before filming, because a brand reading silent captions alone can't picture the video.

Send the actions and the text side by side so the brand sees what is on screen at each moment. Ask them to confirm the captions and whether they want music, original sound or none, and agree any required wording in writing before you film. If they would like a voiceover version too, offer it as a separate cut rather than reshaping this one.
