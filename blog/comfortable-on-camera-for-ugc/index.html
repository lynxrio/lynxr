How do you get comfortable talking to the camera for UGC?

Getting comfortable on camera for UGC comes from repeating small, low-stakes drills rather than waiting to feel ready. Talk to one real person behind the lens, film in short takes you can redo in seconds, and rewatch with a short checklist so you fix one habit at a time instead of judging the whole video.

Published 7 November 2026

Why does talking to a phone feel so awkward at first?

A phone gives you no reaction, so your brain fills the silence with doubt about how you look and sound.

In a normal conversation you get nods, laughs and questions that tell you to keep going. A lens gives you nothing, which is why your voice flattens and your hands freeze. The drills below replace that missing feedback with a listener you imagine, a take short enough to feel safe, and a review you control. None of this is about becoming a different person on camera. A UGC video works best when you sound like yourself telling a friend about something you used.

If you are not sure camera work is for you at all, the answer on making UGC without showing your face covers the options, but it is worth trying these drills first.

Who should you picture when you talk to the camera?

Picture one specific person you know well, who would actually want this tip, and talk to them as if they asked.

Talking to an audience makes you perform. Talking to one friend makes you explain, which is the tone a brand is paying for. Before you press record, say their name in your head and the question they would ask you. Then answer it. If you need a first line to start from, the guide on writing a UGC hook gives you shapes to try.

  • Put a small sticky note with their initial right next to the lens, so your eyes have somewhere to land.
  • Say the first line as a reply, starting with a word like so, honestly or okay, because a reply sounds less rehearsed than an opening.
  • Keep the phone at eye level and about an arm away, so you are looking across at someone rather than down at a screen.
  • Stand or sit the way you would if that friend were in the room, not the way you think a presenter should.

How short should each practice take be?

Keep each take to one line or one beat, because a short take is cheap to redo and you stop fearing the mistake.

Trying to get a whole video in one go puts all the pressure on a single attempt, and one stumble near the end ruins it. Film the hook alone, then the next line, then the next. Say each line three times in a row without stopping the recording: once as written, once faster, once as if you were slightly bored. The bored take is often the most natural one.

Writing your lines in a two-column script helps here, since each row becomes one take.

How do you rewatch your takes without picking yourself apart?

Rewatch with a short checklist and look for one habit to fix, instead of asking whether you like how you look.

Everyone is harsh on their own face and voice, and that judgement tells you nothing you can act on. A checklist turns a feeling into a task. Watch each take once with the sound on and once with it off.

  • Sound on: did any line sound read rather than said?
  • Sound on: where did you rush, and where did you trail off?
  • Sound off: where are your eyes, and do they drift to your own image on the screen?
  • Sound off: what are your hands doing, and does it match what you are saying?
  • Pick one item to change in the next session and ignore the rest for now.

Turning off the preview or covering it with a scrap of paper stops you watching yourself mid-take, which is a common reason eyes drift.

How do you build this into a routine before paid work?

Set a short daily session, film a few lines from a real script, and keep every take so you can compare later sessions with earlier ones.

Practice that has a point is easier to keep doing. Write a short script for something you already own, using the parts in the UGC script template, and film it a line at a time. Delete nothing for the first few weeks. When you play an early take next to a recent one, you will see what changed, and that is more useful than any pep talk.

Some of those practice videos can become samples, which the answers on practising before your first paid job and making samples before a brand hires you cover in detail.
