How do you script a routine video around one product?

Script a routine video by choosing a real routine you already do, then placing the product at the single step it changes. Keep the other steps quick and mostly silent, give the product step the close shot and the full sentences, and end on the finished routine instead of a pitch.

Published 22 November 2026

Which routine should you build the video around?

Pick a routine you already do in real life, because a viewer can tell when the steps were invented for the camera.

A routine video is a format where the shape is the order of the steps, not the product. That matters, because the steps are what make it feel like a normal post. If you have to make up a routine to fit the product in, the viewer has nothing familiar to hold on to. What a UGC video format is explains why the shape can carry a product it was never built for, as long as the shape itself is true.

  • Choose a routine with a clear start and a clear end, such as leaving the house, making a first drink, or a clean-up before bed. A clear end gives the video somewhere to land.
  • Keep it to three to five steps you can film in one place, so you are not moving lights and the phone between every shot.
  • Make sure the product belongs in it. If you would never use it at that moment, pick a different routine rather than forcing it.
  • Do the routine once without filming and write the steps down in order. That list becomes the skeleton of the script.

Where should the product sit in the routine?

Put the product at the one step it changes, and let the steps before it build up to that moment.

If the product appears in the first second and stays in every shot, the video stops being a routine and becomes a demo. If it shows up halfway through, at the step where something used to be slow or annoying, the routine gives it a reason to be there. Each beat before that step should be short and change what is on screen. The parts of a routine still map onto the parts of a UGC video script: the early steps are the setup, the product step is the turn, and the finished routine is the close.

How do you keep the product the focus without it feeling like an ad?

Give the product the only close shot and the only full sentences, and keep every other step quick, wide and nearly silent.

The contrast does the work. When the rest of the routine moves fast, the moment the camera slows down and moves in tells the viewer this is the step that matters, without you saying so. That is the turn in this shape. A get-ready-with-me video works the same way across several products; a routine around one product is stricter, because there is only one place the camera is allowed to linger.

  • Say what you did, not what the product is. A line about pressing a button and walking away reads as a real morning. A list of features reads as an ad.
  • Show the old way once, briefly, before the product step. One shot of the pan in the sink is enough to explain why the step changed.
  • Keep your hands busy with the routine. Holding the product up to the lens breaks the routine for a moment and makes it look staged.
  • Let the product step run a little longer than the rest. A slow pour or a full action shown from start to finish gives the viewer time to look.

What goes wrong in routine videos, and how do you fix it?

Routine videos go wrong when there are too many steps, when the product is everywhere, or when nothing changes at the product step.

  • Too many steps: cut the routine back until the product step arrives near the middle. How long a UGC script should be helps you judge what fits.
  • The product in every shot: take it out of the early steps entirely, so its first appearance is the moment it matters.
  • No change at the product step: if the step looks the same before and after, the video has no turn. Show the slower old way once so there is something to compare against.
  • A pitch at the end: close on the finished routine and one simple line, so the ending feels like the end of your morning rather than the start of an ad.

How do you fit the brand's brief into a routine video?

Put any required lines inside the product step, where you are already talking, so they sound like part of the routine.

Read the brief before you choose the routine, because it may name a moment of the day, a setting, or a line you have to say. If it asks for something the routine cannot hold, ask the brand which matters more before you film, rather than squeezing both in. If you want a method for sorting fixed lines from free ones, turning a brand brief into a script walks through it. Send the step list with the script, so the brand can see where its product sits before you shoot.
