How do you practise UGC before your first paid job?

You practise UGC by running the whole job on products you own: script it, film it, review it against a checklist, then make it again with one thing fixed. Repeating that loop tends to teach you more than filming lots of different videos once, because each round shows you exactly what changed.

Published 6 November 2026

What should you practise on before a brand sends you anything?

Practise on everyday products you already own and use, because you can film them today and speak about them honestly.

Pick something from your kitchen, bathroom or bag that you reach for without thinking. You already know where it lives, what annoyed you before you had it, and how you use it, which is the raw material for a hook and a story. Avoid buying things just to practise, because the point is the routine, not the product.

Rotate between a few product types, such as one thing you apply, one thing you use with your hands and one thing that sits on a shelf. Each one makes you solve a different filming problem, so you are not only rehearsing the shot you are already good at.

What does one practice round look like from start to finish?

One round is five steps: write a short brief for yourself, script it, film it in one sitting, edit it, then review it the next day.

  • Write a pretend brief of three lines: the product, the viewer, and the one thing the video should make them feel or do.
  • Script it with a hook, a few beats and a close, using the parts of a UGC video script as your checklist.
  • Film every line and every shot in one sitting, so you learn how long a full shoot takes you, setup and resets included.
  • Edit a rough cut, then put it away until the next day so you watch it with fresh eyes.
  • Review it against the checklist below and pick the single thing to fix in the next round.

Practising against a written brief matters more than it looks. It trains you to read what a brand asks for before you get creative, and how to turn a brand brief into a script shows the same habit on a real one.

How do you review your own footage without just cringing at it?

Review your footage with a fixed checklist, because a list turns a vague feeling that it looks bad into one thing you can change.

CheckWhat to look forWhat to try next round
The first secondDoes something happen before you speak, or do you start on a still face?Start mid-action, already holding or using the product.
SoundCan you hear every word on your phone speaker with the volume low?Move closer to the phone and film somewhere with soft furnishings.
LightIs your face or the product darker than the background?Turn so the window is in front of you, not behind.
PaceAre there pauses you would scroll past?Cut the gap, or say the line again with less lead-in.
The productCan a viewer tell what it is with the sound off?Add a close shot of it in your hand while you use it.
The closeDoes the video end on one clear ask?Rewrite the last line so it points back to the hook.

Watch once with sound and once without. The silent pass shows whether your shots tell the story on their own, which is what a shot list is meant to make sure of.

Which skills should you drill on their own?

Drill hooks, delivery and close shots separately, because each one improves faster when you repeat it without filming a whole video.

For hooks, write three openings for the same product and film all three back to back. Watching them side by side shows you which one makes you want to keep watching, and how to write a UGC hook that stops the scroll gives you shapes to try.

For delivery, read a line to camera, then say it again without looking at the words. The second take tends to sound closer to how you talk, and that natural delivery is what you are practising for. For close shots, film the product being opened, poured or pressed in one steady take, and keep going until your hands stop shaking the frame.

How do you know when practice is ready to become samples?

Practice is ready to become samples when a round passes your whole checklist and you would be happy for a stranger to watch it.

Keep every round in one folder, named in order, so you can compare your first video with your latest one. Seeing the difference is the clearest sign of what you have learned, and it tells you which skill still needs a drill.
