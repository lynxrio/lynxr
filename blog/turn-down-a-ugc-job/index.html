How do you say no to a UGC job politely?

To turn down a UGC job politely, reply soon, thank the brand by name, give one short honest reason, and say what would make a yes possible next time. Keep it to a few lines, skip the long apology, and leave a specific door open so the brand knows a later job is welcome.

Published 15 November 2026

Do you need to give a reason when you say no?

Give one short reason when it helps the brand plan around you, and keep it general when the real reason would only start an argument.

A reason turns a closed door into useful information. If you say you are booked until [DATE], the brand knows when to come back. If you say the product is outside what you film, they know not to send the next one like it. A reason is not a debate, though. When the honest version is that the brief asks for something you will not do, say that it is not the right fit and stop there.

If you are unsure whether to say no at all, run the offer through how to spot a UGC brand deal that isn't worth taking first. Read the brief properly too, sorting the fixed asks from the flexible ones with how to turn a brand brief into a script, because a brief that looks like too much can have room in it. Decide, then write, because a decline written while you are still wavering tends to read like an opening bid.

What should a polite decline message include?

A polite decline has four parts: thanks, a clear no, one reason, and a door left open, in that order and in a few lines.

  • Thanks that shows you read the offer: mention the product or the idea, not just a generic thank you.
  • A no in the first or second sentence, so nobody has to read to the end to find out where they stand.
  • One reason, said once. Two or three reasons sound like excuses and invite the brand to solve each one.
  • A door that names something real: a date you are free, the kind of product you film, or an offer to hear about the next project.
  • A short sign-off with no apology beyond a single sorry, because a long one makes a reasonable decision sound like a mistake.

Here is the shape in full: Hi [NAME], thank you for thinking of me for [PRODUCT]. I am going to pass on this one, because I am fully booked until [DATE]. I would love to hear about the next project after that, so please keep me in mind. Thanks again, [NAME].

How do you word the no for each kind of reason?

Match the line to the real reason, and match the open door to what would actually change your answer.

When should you offer an alternative instead of a flat no?

Offer an alternative when the problem is the shape of the job rather than the job itself, and you would happily say yes to a different version.

If the deadline is the issue, suggest a later date. If the brief asks for too much in one video, suggest fewer videos or one simpler idea, and let the brand pick. If the product is wrong but the brand is right, name another product of theirs you would film. Each of these hands the decision back to the brand with a real option attached.

Skip the alternative when the reason is a line you will not cross, such as being asked to pretend you have used something you have not. An alternative there sounds like a price for crossing it. A clean, friendly no is kinder to both of you.

What should you leave out of a decline message?

Leave out anything that criticises the brand, over-explains your choice, or promises a yes you might not give later.

  • Criticism of the offer or the product. You can turn it down without telling them what is wrong with it, and the message may be forwarded inside the company.
  • A long account of your week. One reason is enough, and more detail invites them to work around it.
  • Maybe when you mean no. A soft maybe keeps the brand waiting and leaves you writing a second, harder message later.
  • A promise to do the next one. Say you would like to hear about it, which you can keep, rather than that you will say yes, which you might not.
  • Silence. Not replying leaves the brand guessing, so a short no protects the relationship better than none at all.

What do you do after you have turned the job down?

Note the brand, the reason and any date you gave, then follow through on the door you left open when the time comes.

If you said you were booked until a certain date, send one short message once that date arrives, saying you have room now and asking whether anything is coming up. That turns a polite no into the start of the next conversation, and if they go quiet, the timing in following up after a pitch goes unanswered works here too.

If the brand replies with a changed offer, treat it as a new job and judge it on its own terms. Saying no once does not oblige you to say yes to the second version, and saying yes does not undo the first answer.
