What goes in a UGC script for a skincare brand?

A skincare UGC script shows the product in a real routine and promises nothing you have not seen yourself. It opens on a skin situation the viewer recognises, gives the texture a close-up, says honestly how long you have used it, and closes by asking for one thing.

Published 5 October 2026

Why is a skincare UGC script harder to write than most?

Skincare results are slow and vary by person, so a skincare script sells what the camera can see today.

A gadget can be shown working in one take. A moisturiser can't, and a video that pretends otherwise asks the viewer to believe something you haven't filmed. What a camera can show is the texture on a fingertip, how much you use, where the product sits between cleanser and sunscreen, and how your skin looks straight after you put it on.

A skincare brief may also come with words you must use and words you must never say. That list wins over anything that sounds better, so read it before you write the hook. How to turn a brand brief into a script shows how to mark what is fixed and what is yours.

What should the hook of a skincare UGC video be about?

The hook should name a skin situation the viewer is in right now, not a result the product might give them.

A result hook makes a promise the rest of the video can't keep, and a brief that bans result claims won't allow it anyway. A situation hook is true for you and familiar to the viewer, and it sets the product up as the next step in a routine rather than a miracle. The shapes in how to write a UGC hook that stops the scroll all work here, as long as the hook points at the situation.

Result hook to avoidSituation hook to use
“This cleared my skin.”“My skin feels tight by lunch, whatever I put on in the morning.”
“The only moisturiser you'll ever need.”“I've been using three products where I think I need one.”
“Say goodbye to dry patches.”“This is what my cheeks look like under makeup by the afternoon.”
“Your skin will thank you.”“I keep buying creams that feel nice in the jar and greasy by noon.”

What beats does a skincare UGC script need?

A skincare UGC script needs five beats: the situation, the routine, the texture, an honest timeline, and a close that asks for one thing.

Each beat should change what's on screen, so the viewer always has something new to look at while you talk. The parts are the same ones laid out in the parts of a UGC video script; skincare only fixes what goes in each.

PartWhat happens on screenIts job
HookYou name the skin situation, with your bare skin in frame.Makes the viewer check their own skin.
RoutineYou show where the product sits: after cleanser, before sunscreen, or wherever the brief says.Shows you actually use it.
TextureA close shot of the product on a fingertip, then on skin.Shows what no caption can.
TimelineYou say how long you've used it, in plain words.Makes every other line believable.
CloseYou ask for one thing: the link, a save or a comment.Gives the viewer a next step.

In this shape the turn is the texture shot. It's the moment the product stops being a jar in your hand and becomes something the viewer can almost feel, so keep it close, keep it slow, and don't talk over all of it.

Which claims should a skincare UGC script leave out?

Leave out any claim about what the product does to skin unless you have seen it yourself or the brief gives it word for word.

  • Medical words. Treatment language isn't yours to use. Say what you noticed instead: “My skin felt less tight by the end of the week.”
  • Promises about other people. You know your skin, not everyone's. Say who you are: “I have combination skin, and it didn't make my forehead shiny.”
  • A staged before-and-after. If the brief asks for one, film both halves in the same light, at the same angle, after the time has really passed.
  • Ingredient explanations. If the brief names an ingredient, use the brief's words exactly and don't add what it does.

Every one of these is a UGC habit worth keeping on any product, but skincare is where a careless line does the most harm: the viewer is deciding what to put on their face. When a line sits between what you've seen and what the brand wants said, follow the brief.
